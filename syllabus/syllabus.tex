\customsection{Course Description from Course Catalog}  
\noindent The physics of stars is introduced from first principles, leading from
star formation to nuclear fusion to late stellar evolution and the end points of
stars: white dwarfs, neutron stars and black holes. The theory of stellar
structure and evolution is elegant and impressively powerful, bringing together
all branches of physics to predict the life cycles of the stars. The basic
physical processes in the interstellar medium will also be described, and the
role of multi-wavelength astronomy will be used to illustrate our understanding
of the structure of the Milky Way Galaxy, from the massive black hole at the
center to the halo of dark matter which emcompasses it.

\customsection{Student Learning Outcomes} 
\noindent At the end of the class, the students should be able to:
\begin{borderedsquare}
     \setlength\itemsep{0.3em}        
	\item formulate a comphrehensive approach to answering open questions in
	astronomy that includes using physical prinicples and astronomical data to support their arguments.
	\item present research through oral presentations and text format with
	supporting figures.
    \item compute stellar properties from observations.
    \item show how the laws of force equilibrium, and mass and energy
conservation lead to the equations of stellar structure.
    \item relate the present state of a star to its state at any time
in the future.
    \item describe how a stellar companion can alter the evolution of stellar populations.
    \item understand how stars form and be able to demonstrate how the
interstellar medium interacts with the initial and end states of stars. 
    \item be conversant with the current state of research in extrasolar planets
and understand how our solar system is different to and similar to other
detected solar systems.
    \item understand the structure of the Milky Way galaxy, and able to relate
the dark matter distribution to that of stars, gas, dust and black holes.
\end{borderedsquare}

\customsection{Expected Prerequisite Knowledge}
\noindent The expected prerequisite knowledge for this course is the material
covered in `Stars, Galaxies, and the Universe' (33-224) and its prequisites.
Stellar physics builds on a small amount of material from `Quantum Physics'
(33-234) but the required material will also be discussed during this course. 


\customsection{Evaluation Procedures}    
\label{sec:eval-procudures}
\noindent The course will consist of in-class lectures and homeworks based on
the lecture material as well as two long-term projects. There will be no
mid-term or final exams. Instead, each project will be evaluated based on two
in-class presentations. 

\textbf{Homework:} Homework will be given approximately weekly. The homework
will be shorter than for most courses because you will be spending time every
week on projects. Science is a collaborative endeavour. To this end, you are
encouraged to work with your colleagues on homework and can obtain a maximum of
5 pts extra per homework by citing those who you worked with. 

\textbf{Projects: } Two long-term projects will be carried out during the
semester. Each will count for on third of the course grade. The projects will be
chosen from a list, and will be graded based on an update presentation roughly
halfway through (after $\sim 1$ month), a final presentation, and a short paper
writeup. The project grading rules are given at the end of this syllabus. There
will be time at the halfway point of each class (``Project Workshop'') to ask
the instructor questions about projects, brainstorm with fellow students, or
carry out research.\\

\customsection{Recommended Software and Tools}  
\noindent All of the projects will have a computational component. While there
is not a perferred operating system for any of the projects, it is strongly
recommended that students use a version control system like \texttt{git},

students working as a team on a project to facilitate sharing of code and data.
